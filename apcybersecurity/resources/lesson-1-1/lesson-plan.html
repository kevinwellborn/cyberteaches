Lesson 1.1: Understanding Social Engineering

Time 2 class periods (about 100 minutes)
Unit 1 · Introduction to Security
Online Topic 1.1, 2 CyberTeaches videos, Phishing Spotter lab, Phishing Investigation mission

Overview

Most successful attacks start with a person, not a computer. Students learn how social engineers use psychological levers such as urgency, authority and scarcity to get people to click a link, share a password or hold a door open. They analyze real-looking phishing messages, name the tactic and the warning signs, and practice the most important defense: stop, verify through a separate trusted channel, and report.

Learning objectives

Students will be able to:

  1. Define social engineering and explain why attackers target people instead of technology.
  2. Identify common social engineering attacks: phishing, spear phishing, smishing, vishing, pretexting, baiting and tailgating.
  3. Name the psychological lever (urgency, authority, scarcity, fear, curiosity, helpfulness or consensus) used in a message.
  4. Find the warning signs in a suspicious email, text or phone call.
  5. Recommend a response that verifies the request through a separate, trusted channel.

AP skills practiced

  • Detect Attacks: spot the warning signs of a phishing message.
  • Analyze Risk: explain what could happen if a person falls for the attack.
  • Mitigate Risk: choose the best action to stop the attack.

  • Try the Phishing Spotter lab and the Phishing Investigation mission yourself (about 15 minutes).
  • Optional: save one real phishing email from your school spam folder, with names removed, to show on Day 1.
  • Remind students never to click real suspicious links. Today's messages are all safe practice copies.

Assessment

  • Formative: guided practice, classwork Parts A to D, the exit ticket.
  • Video check: the notes students write during the video and the homework scam story.
  • Online: Phishing Spotter lab, Phishing Investigation mission and the Topic 1.1 AP-style questions (instant feedback).
  • Success criteria: the student names the lever and two warning signs in a new message, and recommends verifying through a separate channel.

Common misconceptions

  • "Phishing always has bad spelling." AI now writes flawless messages; check the request, not the grammar.
  • "A padlock (HTTPS) means the site is safe." It only means the connection is encrypted.
  • "Replying to ask if it is real is enough." The attacker controls the reply. Verify another way.
  • "Only emails are phishing." Texts, calls, QR codes and in-person visits are social engineering too.

Career connection

Security awareness trainers, SOC analysts and help-desk technicians investigate reported phishing every day. Part of their job is exactly today's mission: trace a link and warn users.

Differentiation

  • Support: give students the list of seven levers as a reference card; let them work Part A with a partner.
  • Extension: students write their own (harmless) phishing example for a classmate to analyze, labeling every warning sign on the back.
  • Videos: turn on YouTube captions (auto-translate works for many languages); students can rewatch at home or at a slower speed.
  • Accessibility: the online labs work with keyboard and screen zoom; slides can be printed as handouts.

Supports for English learners

  • Visuals: annotate the phishing email on the board with arrows and icons for each warning sign.
  • Word wall: post the vocabulary with the Spanish words.
  • Partners: pair students to read scenarios aloud; first-language talk is welcome.
  • Sentence frames: “This message uses ___ because ___.” “A warning sign is ___.” “I would verify by ___.”
  • Classwork Part E uses matching, a word bank and fill-in sentences.
